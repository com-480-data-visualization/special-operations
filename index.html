Is Europe really falling behind?

Not in the simple way the headline suggests. Since 2000, European economies kept growing, but public markets compounded much more aggressively in the United States and selected Asian markets.

Read the charts from left to right: first the real economy, then the investable market proxy, then the global index weight that investors actually experience.

Key findings

Europe grew. Markets repriced the world elsewhere.

Act 1 · Real economy

Europe did not stop growing.

Start with GDP in regional mode. By 2023, Europe is around 2.48x its 2000 GDP baseline, and GDP per capita also more than doubled. The first lesson is that the story is not collapse.

Act 2 · Market proxy

The market line tells a harsher story.

Switch to country ETF prices and the gap opens. Germany and the UK sit below their 2000 ETF baselines, while the US ETF more than doubles and Korea becomes the key counterexample.

Act 3 · Uneven gap

Prosperity and markets separate.

The scatter view fixes the comparison: GDP per capita growth on one axis, ETF growth on the other. Countries to the upper right turned prosperity into investable market returns; Europe is more mixed.

Listed-company value compounds elsewhere.

Market capitalization makes the asymmetry harder to miss. The US compounds strongly, Korea is exceptional, and several major European markets trail despite real-economy growth.

2008: tipping point?

After 2008, Europe’s market proxies diverged more than its output.

Rebuilt on a constant-PPP basis, the story is more precise: Europe does lag in real GDP per person, but the much larger post-2008 split is in valuation proxies and household capital gains.

1. Market verdict

Post-2008, market-linked proxies tell the sharpest version of the story. Europe’s ETF proxy remains weak while the US proxy rebounds strongly.

Source: country and regional ETF and market-cap proxies, converted to a constant-2021 PPP-equivalent basis, 2008-2023.

2. Fundamentals held up better

Macro growth slows after 2008, but it does not collapse. On a real/PPP basis, Europe still grows in GDP and GDP per capita; the market-proxy gap is larger.

Source: processed World Bank GDP and GDP per capita series, compared with ETF-price growth.

3. Growth for whom?

Distribution question starts with assets. If US households with financial wealth captured much larger valuation gains than euro-area households, faster growth may have favored capital owners first.

ECB comparison uses average annual capital gains on household financial assets since 2000, expressed as share of annual gross disposable income.

Source: ECB, The Asset Holdings of Euro Area Households, latest observations Q2 2025.

Read so far

Current read after rebuilding the data: Europe’s real-output gap is present but moderate; the sharper divergence is in valuation proxies and capital-gains capture.

Next data pass should test whether stronger US market growth translated into broader household welfare, or mainly into asset-owner gains.

Methodology: the main comparison covers 20 countries with comparable country ETF histories from 2000 to 2023. Growth mode normalizes each country to its year-2000 baseline. Absolute mode is shown only where units are comparable; ETF prices remain a real/PPP-adjusted investable-market proxy.

Market capitalization comes from World Bank listed-company market cap data where reliable. Recent gaps are filled with the matching iShares MSCI country ETF price as a proxy; Russia, China, India, and Sweden are excluded from the market-cap comparison because of spotty data.
