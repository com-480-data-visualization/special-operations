Is Europe really falling behind?

Europe was long seen as one of the world’s economic powerhouses. As other regions accelerate and the narrative of European decline grows louder, understanding its trajectory matters for citizens, policymakers, and entrepreneurs trying to anticipate where economic power will lie if nothing changes.

Follow the argument from the real economy to market proxies, from regional averages to country variation, and finally to the industries behind global index concentration.

Key findings

Europe grew. Markets repriced the world elsewhere.

Act 1 · Real economy

Europe did not stop growing.

Start with GDP in regional mode. By 2023, Europe is around 1.38x its 2000 GDP baseline, and GDP per capita is around 1.21x. Any claim that Europe is "falling behind" has to explain why this compounded growth is not enough.

1.38x Europe GDP 1.21x Europe GDP per capita

Act 2 · Why US and Europe?

The benchmark is not arbitrary.

The United States and Europe are both high-income, institutionally mature markets that matter in global portfolios. Comparing them lets us separate a real-economy question from a listed-market question: did people get poorer, or did public equity value compound somewhere else?

Act 3 · Divergence hypothesis

The gap opens when we change the measure.

The central hypothesis is not that Europe stopped producing output. It is that output growth translated less cleanly into public-market returns. The scatter view makes that split visible: prosperity growth on one axis, ETF growth on the other.

2.51x US ETF 0.90x Germany ETF 0.62x UK ETF

Act 4 · Inside Europe

Europe is not one datapoint.

The European average hides national variation, but the selected country scatter gives a sharper read: the US remains above the European cluster on the market proxy, while Switzerland is the main European exception rather than proof that Europe moves as one block.

0.43x-1.15x Europe ETF range 1.52x US ETF Switzerland main exception

Read: Europe is heterogeneous, but no selected European country carries the market story the way the US does. The gap is not only a single weak country dragging down the average.

Act 5 · Industry drivers

The winning sectors sit elsewhere.

The ACWI snapshots translate the country story into sector exposure. From 2015 to 2026, global index weight becomes more concentrated in the United States, and information technology becomes the dominant sector block.

63.7% US ACWI weight 30.7% information technology

Conclusion · What falling behind means

Europe is not collapsing. Listed-market power moved elsewhere.

The final scatter keeps the comparison narrow: selected Europe versus the US. Output per person is not wildly different, but the market proxy is. Europe did not stop growing; the listed-market upside concentrated elsewhere.

1.18x-1.26x Europe GDP/person 0.43x-1.15x Europe ETF 1.52x US ETF
Final read

The evidence does not support a simple European collapse story. It supports a more specific claim: Europe kept growing, but the investable market story shifted toward the US, especially through listed equity concentration and technology-heavy sectors.

The limitation is equally important: these charts measure output and listed capital, not the full well-being of households.

Wrap up

Europe is not simply falling behind. It is falling behind in a specific market story.

The evidence supports a narrower thesis: Europe continued to grow, but the investable-market rewards, sector concentration, and global index weight shifted more strongly toward the United States. The final question is whether these indicators track broad well-being, or mostly the returns to listed capital.

What the data can say

GDP and GDP per capita reject a simple stagnation story. ETF, market-cap, and ACWI views show the sharper divergence.

What it cannot fully say

Market data does not measure public services, inequality, household balance sheets, or lived well-being directly.

How to read the project

Treat the guided story as the argument and the controls as the audit trail: change the indicator, year, and geography to look for exceptions.

Methodology: the main comparison covers 20 countries with comparable country ETF histories from 2000 to 2023. Compounded mode normalizes each country or region to its year-2000 baseline. Absolute mode is shown only where units are comparable; ETF prices remain a real/PPP-adjusted investable-market proxy.

Market capitalization comes from World Bank listed-company market cap data where reliable. Recent gaps are filled with the matching iShares MSCI country ETF price as a proxy; Russia, China, India, and Sweden are excluded from the market-cap comparison because of spotty data.
